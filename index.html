Raising a kid who’s willing to try.

Our feeding plan — built on the Division of Responsibility. The goal isn’t broccoli tonight. It’s a child who stays curious about food.

29 months 4 min read No guilt, no bribes

The foundation

One rule holds up everything else

Ellyn Satter’s Division of Responsibility — the most-studied feeding framework there is.

We decideWhat food is served, when, and where.
She decidesWhether she eats it, and how much.
  • Our job ends when the food hits the table. No convincing, no bargaining, no “three more bites.”
  • Pressure — even kind, cheerful pressure — is the best-documented way to shrink a toddler’s diet. It turns new food into a test.
  • She has to feel genuinely free to say no. That freedom is what eventually makes yes possible.
Week one

Six things to change now

Structure does most of the work. These change the environment, not her.

  • 1
    Lock the schedule. 3 meals + 2 snacks, every 2.5–3 hours. Only water in between.
  • 2
    End the grazing. A toddler who nibbles all day is never hungry enough to be curious. Milk with meals; cap around 16 oz a day.
  • 3
    Serve family style. Bowls on the table, she serves herself. Self-serving hands her the “how much” — which is what lets her be brave about the “what.”
  • 4
    Every plate = one safe food + the family meal. Cottage cheese, PB toast, or a slice of pizza is always the anchor.
  • 5
    No short-order cooking. One meal. The safe food is part of it — not a rescue plate made after she refuses.
  • 6
    We eat the same food, at the same time, and say nothing about her eating. Modeling beats instruction at this age.
The safe-food anchor is the whole trick. It guarantees she won’t go hungry, which takes the fear out of it for us — and our tension is what she reads at the table.
Rhythm

The weekly exposure plan

Acceptance usually takes 10–15+ neutral exposures. Most families stop at three and decide she hates it. Repetition without pressure is the active ingredient.

3–4 nights in a row

One target food at dinner

Same food, several nights. Then rotate next week. Repeating one food beats variety.

Every serving

A “no-thank-you” portion

One bite’s worth. A big pile reads as a demand; a crumb reads as optional.

Every dinner

The learning plate

A small side plate for touching, licking, dissecting, spitting out. Not eating it still counts.

2× a week · 5 min

Kitchen time

Washing, stirring, tearing lettuce. Handling food raises acceptance long before it’s eaten.

1× a week

Her grocery pick

She chooses one item. We cook it. Zero obligation to eat it.

Whenever

Messy play

Hands in it, away from the table. Texture aversion fades through touch, not tasting.

Track exposures, not bites. “Tomatoes: served 7 times” is progress. “She ate one” is luck.
At the table

Do this, not that

Do

  • Describe, don’t sell. “This carrot is crunchy and sweet.”
  • Eat it yourself, visibly, with enjoyment.
  • Accept “no” the first time, without comment.
  • Let her spit it out into a napkin. Escape routes make trying safe.
  • Pair new foods with favorites, never in place of them.
  • Keep meals to ~20 minutes and end them calmly.
  • Let her get a little hungry. Hunger is an ally.

Don’t

  • Don’t use dessert as payment. It teaches that vegetables are the price of real food.
  • Don’t praise eating. “Good girl, you ate it!” turns food into performance.
  • Don’t rely on hidden vegetables. Fine for nutrition, useless for learning.
  • Don’t negotiate bites.
  • Don’t make a backup meal.
  • Don’t comment on her eating — good or bad.
  • Don’t let one rough meal change the plan. Intake averages out over a week, not a day.
The bridges

Food chaining

Move one variable at a time — flavor, or texture, or shape — so each new food is only slightly unfamiliar. Big jumps get refused. Small ones get eaten. Tap to open.

🍳Cottage cheese → eggs❯
cottage cheese→ricotta→ricotta on toast→soft egg curds→scrambled egg→egg salad→hard-boiled white

Same lumpy-soft mouthfeel, flavor shifting slowly. Egg curds are the key rung — they feel like cottage cheese.

🍕Pizza → meat, sauce & greens❯
cheese pizza→+ minced pepperoni→bigger meat pieces→meatball on the side→chicken meatball→cut chicken
pizza sauce→marinara for dipping→tomato soup→roasted tomato→raw tomato
pizza + wilted spinach→cheesy toast + spinach→sautéed spinach

Pizza is the best base we have — already bread + sauce + dairy + topping. Add one topping at a time, cut small.

🥜Peanut butter → new textures❯
PB on toast→PB on crackers→PB on apple→PB on celery→PB dip for carrots
peanut butter→almond / sunflower butter→hummus→bean dip

PB is a texture ladder and a dip. Dips work because she controls how much touches the food.

🍓Fruit & the wet / crunchy problem❯
fruit pouch→applesauce→warm cooked apple→roasted apple slice→raw apple
freeze-dried strawberry→fresh strawberry→melon→orange segment

Wet and crunchy are harder than soft and dry. Freeze-dried fruit is the standard bridge into juicy fruit.

🥦Vegetables, easiest first❯
roasted sweet potato→roasted carrot→broccoli “trees”→peas→cucumber coins→pepper strips→leafy greens

Start sweet and roasted, not steamed and plain. Salt and olive oil are allowed — we’re teaching her to like vegetables, not to eat them virtuously.

🍗Meat — last and hardest❯
meatball→chicken meatball→nugget→shredded chicken in sauce→chicken thigh→ground beef→steak strips

Meat is chewy and unpredictable. Ground and moist first; dry chicken breast almost last. A dipping sauce helps a lot.

When to get help

Red flags

Ordinary picky eating is a phase. These suggest a feeding disorder — which needs an evaluation, not more patience.

  • Fewer than ~20 accepted foods, or a list that keeps shrinking with foods dropping off for good.
  • Whole textures or food groups refused — no proteins at all, or nothing that requires chewing.
  • Gagging, choking, coughing, or vomiting at meals; pocketing food in her cheeks; chewing endlessly without swallowing.
  • Weight loss, or falling off her growth curve.
  • Real distress — panic or gagging just from seeing a food, or from it being on the table.
  • Still on mostly purées, or refusing all age-appropriate textures at 29 months.
  • Signs of reflux, constipation, or pain. Kids who hurt when they eat stop eating — treat the pain first.
  • Picky eating alongside speech delay or broad sensory sensitivity (clothing, noise, mess).
If any of these fit, ask the pediatrician for a referral to a pediatric feeding therapist (SLP or OT). Early help is short help — this is not an overreaction.
The long game

What progress actually looks like

Roughly this order. It can take months, and sliding backward for a week is normal.

  • Tolerates it on the table → then on her plate
  • Touches it → picks it up → smells it
  • Licks it → puts it in her mouth → spits it out
  • Chews and swallows one bite
  • Eats it sometimes → eats it
Every step before swallowing is real progress. Spitting it out is a win — she tried it.
