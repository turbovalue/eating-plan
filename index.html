Bite by Bite

Ten steps to a kid who’ll try things.

The goal isn’t broccoli tonight. It’s a child who stays curious about food.

The plan

tap any step
We decideWhat is served, when, and where.
They decideWhether they eat, and how much.
1Set the schedule❯

Three meals and two snacks, every 2½ to 3 hours. Water only in between. Milk with meals, about 16 oz a day.

A toddler who grazes all day is never hungry enough to be curious.

2Cook one meal❯

No short-order cooking, no backup plate. Everyone eats the same thing.

If a second option is coming, the first one never gets learned.

3A safe food on every plate❯

Cottage cheese, peanut butter toast, or a slice of pizza — as part of the meal, not a rescue after a refusal.

This is the whole trick. It guarantees nobody goes hungry, which takes the fear out of it for you — and your tension is what gets read at the table.

4Serve family style❯

Bowls on the table. They serve themselves, with help.

Handing over the “how much” is what makes it safe to be brave about the “what.”

5One new food, one bite’s worth❯

Next to a favorite, never instead of one.

A pile reads as a demand. A crumb reads as optional.

6Say nothing about their eating❯

Not praise, not pressure. Describe the food if you like — “this carrot is crunchy and sweet” — then drop it.

  • No “three more bites”
  • No dessert as payment
  • No “good job, you ate it!”
  • No negotiating

Any comment turns eating into a performance for you.

7Accept “no” the first time❯

Cheerfully, without comment. Let them spit it out into a napkin.

An escape route is what makes trying safe in the first place.

8Eat it yourself, visibly❯

Same food, same table, obvious enjoyment. Keep the meal to about 20 minutes and end it calmly.

At this age, modeling beats instruction every time.

9Serve it 10–15 times❯

Same food, several nights running, before you decide anything. Count how often you serve it, not how much gets eaten.

Most families quit at three and conclude it’s hopeless.

10Bridge from what they love❯

Change one thing at a time — flavor, or texture, or shape. Big jumps get refused; small ones get eaten.

🍳Cottage cheese → eggs

cottage cheese→ricotta→soft egg curds→scrambled egg→egg salad

Egg curds are the key rung — they feel like cottage cheese.

🍕Pizza → meat and sauce

cheese pizza→minced pepperoni→meatball→chicken meatball→cut chicken
pizza sauce→marinara dip→tomato soup→roasted tomato

🥜Peanut butter → new textures

PB on toast→on crackers→on apple→as a dip→hummus

🥦Vegetables, easiest first

roasted sweet potato→carrot→broccoli→peas→raw

Sweet and roasted, not steamed and plain. Salt and oil are allowed.

🍗Meat, last and hardest

meatball→nugget→shredded in sauce→chicken thigh→steak strips

Ground and moist first. Dry chicken breast almost last.

When to call the pediatrician❯

Ordinary picky eating is a phase. These aren’t.

  • Fewer than ~20 accepted foods, or the list keeps shrinking
  • A whole food group or texture refused — no proteins, or nothing that needs chewing
  • Gagging, choking, or vomiting at meals; pocketing food in the cheeks
  • Weight loss, or falling off the growth curve
  • Panic or gagging just from seeing a food
  • Signs of reflux, constipation, or pain — kids who hurt when they eat stop eating

Ask for a referral to a pediatric feeding therapist (SLP or OT). Early help is short help.

Why this plan and not another❯

Steps 1–8 are Ellyn Satter’s Division of Responsibility, step 9 is repeated neutral exposure, step 10 is food chaining. They don’t conflict — the last two only decide what goes on the table, which is our job anyway.

Other approaches

Responsive Feeding Therapy

The broader umbrella the Division of Responsibility sits under — individualized rather than one protocol. Rejects rewards, pressure, and ABA-style methods.

This is the training to look for in a therapist.

Reward-based tasting

A sticker for tasting one food, in short sessions away from meals.

A randomized trial found stickers plus repeated tasting beat control on intake and liking at three months, while praise did not. It contradicts step 6, so we’re not using it — but it’s the fallback if a food stays stuck for months.

SOS Approach

A therapist-led ladder of about thirty steps, from tolerating a food in the room to swallowing it.

Built for real sensory aversion. Needs a clinician; evidence is described as emerging and limited.

Behavioral and ABA feeding programs

The spoon doesn’t leave until the bite is taken.

Strongest effect sizes of anything here, but built for tube weaning and failure to thrive. Outside that, the responsive camp considers it flooding.
